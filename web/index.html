An algorithmic impact assessment & regulatory review

From Audit to Accountability

A risk score used by US hospitals predicted who would cost the most, and was used to decide who was sickest. We re-run the audit, then ask the harder questions: who should have stopped it, what assessment should have come first, and would the law in the US, the EU or India require one today?

§01
Problem definition

A score that measures spending, used as if it measured sickness

Hospitals and insurers run high-risk care-management programmes: extra nurses, priority appointments and closer follow-up for the patients most likely to deteriorate. Places are scarce, so patients are ranked by a commercial risk score. In the system studied by Obermeyer and colleagues, anyone at or above the 97th percentile was identified for enrolment automatically, and anyone above the 55th was flagged to their primary-care doctor. Tools in this class are applied to roughly 200 million people in the US each year.

Naming the vendorThe Science paper does not name the product. It was identified in press coverage and in a New York regulators' letter as Optum's Impact Pro.

The score was trained to predict next year's medical cost. Cost was treated as a proxy for health need, on the reasonable-sounding logic that sicker people use more care. But in the US, Black patients with the same illness burden receive less care and so generate less spending, because of unequal access, insurance, distance, time off work and trust. A model that predicts cost accurately therefore ranks Black patients as healthier than they are. Race was never an input; the bias came from the choice of label.

The bias is now well known. What remains open is the governance question this project addresses: what should a deploying hospital have done before switching the tool on, who is accountable for the harm, and would current US, EU and Indian frameworks have forced anyone to look?

§02
Objectives

Five questions, from the data to the statute book

  1. Reproduce the auditRe-implement the original diagnostics on the public synthetic cohort, including the 2022 code correction, and check our numbers against the authors' own.
  2. Isolate the labelTrain one model class on three labels with identical features to show the bias comes from what is predicted, not from the model.
  3. Design the missing assessmentSpecify the algorithmic impact assessment a hospital should have run before deployment, with measurable pass criteria.
  4. Assign accountabilityMap who is responsible and who is accountable across the vendor, hospital, clinicians, payer, patients and regulators.
  5. Test the lawCode US, EU and Indian obligations for 2019 and 2026 and ask whether each would have required the assessment that was missing.
§03
Background & context

How a cost prediction becomes a care decision

Population-health risk scores sit between insurance claims and the clinic. A vendor trains a model on historical claims; a hospital or payer licenses it, picks enrolment thresholds and passes the flagged list to care managers. Each hand-off is a place where someone could have asked what the score actually measures. Nobody in the chain was required to.

    §05
    Methodology & analysis

    Re-running the audit, step by step

    The original patient data cannot be shared, so the authors released a synthetic cohort generated with the synthpop package: patient-years, of them Black (), with the commercial score, programme enrolment, costs, chronic-condition counts, biomarkers and year t − 1 features . It reproduces the structure of the original, not its exact magnitudes, so every figure below is shown next to the published value where one exists.

    ReproducibilityAll numbers on this page are generated by analysis/run_all.py and read from JSON at build time. The raw CSV is downloaded on demand and never redistributed.
    1. Calibration audit. Compare mean health and mean cost across score percentiles by race. The within-percentile gap is estimated by OLS with percentile fixed effects, with 1,000-draw bootstrap intervals.
    2. Mechanism. Compare spending for Black and White patients with the same number of active chronic conditions.
    3. Biomarkers. Repeat the calibration check on HbA1c, blood pressure, creatinine, hematocrit and LDL.
    4. Counterfactual swap. Re-implement the authors' exercise, both as published and with the 2022 correction.
    5. Label choice. Fit LASSO (10-fold CV, standardised features, race excluded) on a 60/40 split to predict log total cost, log avoidable cost, and active chronic conditions.
    6. Selection fairness. At each threshold, compare who is selected with who is truly high-need: the same number of patients ranked by realised chronic conditions.

    The two panels are the whole story in miniature. On cost, the lines overlap: the score is well calibrated across races, which is exactly how a vendor would validate it. On health, the Black line sits above the White line at every percentile. Among patients at or above the 97th percentile, Black patients carry active chronic conditions against for White patients, more (published: 4.8 vs 3.8, +26%).

    Did human review correct the score?

    Enrolment was ultimately a human decision: patient-years were enrolled, of them from the flagged pool. At the same score percentile, Black patients were enrolled at a rate only higher (95% CI to ). The enrolled Black patients still had more chronic conditions on average than enrolled White patients ( vs ). A human in the loop was present but did not act as a safeguard: the higher bar for Black patients survived review.

    §07
    Proposed solution & recommendations

    A pre-deployment impact assessment with a gate the score would have failed

    We propose a seven-gate Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) for population-health risk tools. It combines the impact levels of Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making , the participatory stages of the Ada Lovelace Institute's NHS pilot, the four-step Algorithmic Bias Playbook and NIST's Govern–Map–Measure–Manage cycle . The decisive addition is G1, label validity, a test that checks whether the training target measures need equally across groups. It takes an afternoon with data the hospital already holds.

    Impact levelUnder Canada's four-level scale, a tool that rations access to care for large numbers of patients, with effects that are hard to reverse, scores Level III–IV. That level triggers peer review, published assessments and human decision-making.

        §09
        Conclusion

        Detection was never the hard part

        Limitations

        The cohort is synthetic: it preserves the structure of the original data but not its exact magnitudes, and it has no patient identifiers, so intervals treat patient-years as independent. Chronic-condition counts are themselves shaped by access to care. "White" in the source data means all non-Black patients. The regulatory coding is our legal reading as of 7 October 2026, not legal advice, and several of the instruments cited were still changing when we checked them.
